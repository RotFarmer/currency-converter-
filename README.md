# Currency Converter

A currency converter for your phone. Set a default currency once, and every price you type in or photograph is converted to it. Foreign text in photos is translated too. It comes with thirteen whole-app themes, from the default mech-HUD look to an animated aurora, medieval parchment, the Iron Beetle swamp, an autumn forest, the ocean and Windows 95. On Android it is also a real app with three home-screen widgets: a keypad converter, a quick reference table and a one-tap live camera.

- **Swipe between pages.** Swipe left or right anywhere on a page to go to the next or previous one (Convert, Scan, Settings). The page follows your finger. The quick reference tables, text fields and drag handles keep their own swipes.
- **Home currency.** You choose it on first launch (with a suggestion from your phone's region) and it's remembered. The converter, the quick-reference table and scanned prices all convert to it.
- **"Price in" follows your location.** It's set to the currency of the country you're in: from GPS if you allow location, otherwise from your phone's time zone, which updates automatically when you travel. Picking "Price in" yourself turns this off; switch it back on in Settings.
- **Quick reference tabs.** Tables of amounts, always shown in your home currency: one tab for your "Price in" currency and one for each currency in the ☰ drawer. Swipe left/right or tap a tab to switch; tap a row to put that amount in the converter. Tap **Edit** to choose the amounts for the currency on screen: remove the ones you don't need, type your own, or add back suggested ones. Each currency keeps its own list, and **Reset** brings back the suggested amounts.
- **Up to 10 other currencies.** Tap ☰ **Other currencies** under your home currency to open a drawer with your extra currencies. Tap **+** to add one (up to 10) and **×** to remove one. Drag a currency by its handle (⋮⋮) to put them in your own order; the quick reference tabs move with it as you drag, and the widgets follow it too.
- **Type in any currency.** Every amount, including those in the ☰ drawer, can be typed into; all the others update. 0 in any of them makes them all 0.
- **Live camera.** Point the in-app camera at price tags or a menu and converted prices appear on screen as you move. A price has to be read in more than one frame before it shows, and where two readings of the same spot disagree the one read most often wins, so misreads don't flicker by. In the Android app the phone's own text reader does this several times a second, and a **Prices / Translate** switch lays the translation over each line of a menu or sign, live. Tap the shutter for a full scan with translation. The camera takes on your theme: its labels, buttons, shutter and viewfinder (HUD keeps its dark look).
- **Scan photos.** Take a photo (or pick one) of a price tag, menu or receipt. Each price is found and covered with its converted amount, right on the photo. Tap a price to fix a misread number or change its currency.
- **Translates what it reads.** Menu items and signs are translated into your language. The **Text** view lays the translations over the photo, each price in the list shows its translated item name, and a list of every line is shown below the photo.
- **Works out the currency from the language.** Japanese text means yen, Thai means baht, Hebrew means shekels, and so on. It reads Latin-alphabet languages plus Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Thai, Hebrew, Arabic, Russian/Ukrainian, Greek and Hindi. If it guesses the language wrong, tap **LANG** to pick it.
- **Understands currency signs.** It recognises €, £, ¥, $, ₪, ₹, kr, zł, 円, 원, บาท, ש״ח, руб, codes like `USD` or `CHF`, and formats like `1.280,50 €` or `12.-`. A price without a sign uses the currency most other prices on the same photo show, then the language's currency, then your "Prices in" setting.
- **Install prompt.** On first visit it offers to install itself: a real install button on Android/Chrome, step-by-step Add to Home Screen instructions on iPhone (Apple doesn't let websites install themselves).
- **Works offline.** Install it to your home screen. Exchange rates are saved on the phone, and the text reader is cached after its first download. Translation needs a connection.
- **Private.** Text is read on the phone itself: in the Android app with Google's on-device ML Kit (much faster and more accurate, for Latin, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Devanagari text), and otherwise with [Tesseract.js](https://github.com/naptha/tesseract.js), which also reads Thai, Hebrew, Arabic, Cyrillic and Greek. Photos are never uploaded. The app translates on the phone too; elsewhere only the recognised text is sent to the translator.

It's a web app (PWA), so it runs on iPhone and Android with no app store and no build step.

## Using it on your phone

1. Open **https://rotfarmer.github.io/currency-converter-/** in Safari (iPhone) or Chrome (Android).
2. Install it when the prompt appears, or any time with the **Install** button at the top:
   - **iPhone:** tap Share (on newer iPhones, ••• first), then **Add to Home Screen**.
   - **Android:** tap **Install**.
3. Choose your **home currency** when asked. Allow location if you'd like GPS to set the local currency (without it, the phone's time zone is used).
4. Type an amount on the **Convert** tab, or open **Scan** and use the **Live camera**. Allow camera access when asked.

The live view reads prices with a sign (¥, €, $ …) about once a second. Prices written with a local currency word, such as 1,080円, are picked up by the full scan after you tap the shutter.

Tips for scanning: get close enough that prices fill a good part of the photo, hold steady and use decent light. The first scan downloads the text reader (about 6 MB). A photo in a non-Latin script also downloads that script's model (1–3 MB) the first time.

Settings → Language has switches for translation and for guessing the currency from the language, and lets you pick the language to translate into.

## Android app and widget

On Android you can install Converter as a real app, with three home-screen widgets.

1. On your phone, open **https://github.com/RotFarmer/currency-converter-/releases/download/android-latest/Converter.apk**. It downloads the newest version.
2. Open the downloaded file. The first time, Android asks you to allow installing apps from your browser; allow it, then tap **Install**.
3. To add a widget: long-press an empty spot on your home screen, tap **Widgets**, find **Converter** and drag one onto the screen. There are three:
   - **Converter**: a keypad. Type a price in the currency of the country you're in and see it in your home currency right away. **⇄** flips the direction, **⌫** deletes a digit and **C** clears. Tap the amount to open the app.
   - **Quick reference**: your quick reference table for the currency of the country you're in, with the amounts you chose in the app (Edit on Quick reference). Make it taller to see more rows.
   - **Scan**: tap it to open the app straight into the live camera. Below the camera button it lists the prices from your last scan (live camera or photo) with their item names, converted to your home currency with the latest rates.

   You can also long-press the app icon and tap **Live camera**.

   The widgets take on your theme: its colours, its art (the forest and snow for Iron Beetle, a sunset grid for Vapor, lined paper for Notebook, gingham and lace for Cottage, parchment and corner flourishes for Medieval, and so on), its frame and border, a matching font and the shape of its keys. Iron Beetle's widgets are a dark forest green and show the country at the top. They follow your phone's light or dark mode, whatever the app itself is set to, and on Android 12 and newer they switch by themselves the moment your phone does. 90s, Vapor and 8-bit, which only come in one mode in the app, get a widget look for the other: a charcoal 90s window, a pastel-dawn Vapor and a handheld-green 8-bit. When "Price in" follows your location, they switch to the new country's currency when your phone changes time zone, even if you don't open the app. At home they use the first currency in your ☰ drawer. Tap the time in the corner (for example **3h ⟳**) to refresh the rates.

Text in the app and its widgets stays the size it was designed at, whatever font size your phone is set to.

The app opens the same site as the web version, so changes to the app's features arrive by themselves. Changes to the app itself or its widgets come as new builds, and the app installs those too: it checks GitHub when it opens and every few hours, shows **Version … is ready** on the Convert tab and in Settings → App, and installs it when you tap **Install update**. Android asks you to confirm the first one (and to allow Converter to install updates); after that, on Android 12 and newer, updates install by themselves shortly after you leave the app. This needs builds signed with the same key, so set up the `ANDROID_SIGNING_PASSWORD` secret below; until then the app can only tell you a new build exists. The widgets refresh rates every few hours on their own, even if you don't open the app.

If the app ever closes unexpectedly, it saves what went wrong: **Settings → App** then shows **The app closed unexpectedly**, and tapping it copies the details for a bug report. A widget that fails to draw with its theme's art is drawn plainly instead, and that's shown there too. (App builds 8 and older could close when a widget failed to draw, so the site stops sending them widget data; reinstall to get the widgets updating again.)

**Updates and the signing key.** Android only installs an update over the old app if both are signed with the same key. To give the app a permanent key, add a repository secret once: on GitHub, open the repository's **Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions → New repository secret**, name it `ANDROID_SIGNING_PASSWORD`, and give it a long password you make up. The next build creates the key, locked with that password, and saves it (encrypted) in `android-app/signing/`. Until then each build is signed with a temporary key, so to install a newer build you remove the old app first, which also clears its settings.

How it's built: `android-app/` is a [Capacitor](https://capacitorjs.com) project. The widgets are native Android code in `android-app/android/app/src/main/java/` (`ConverterWidget.java`, `ReferenceWidget.java`, `ScanWidget.java`, with shared code in `Widgets.java` and `WidgetData.java`); the web app sends them data through a small plugin (`WidgetBridgePlugin.java`, called from `sendWidgetData` in `js/app.js`). Theme art for the widgets is drawn from `js/widget-art.js` (generated, loaded only in the app): the app turns each theme's SVG art into images, a light and a dark set, and `WidgetArt.java` composes them at each widget's size. The theme's colours for both modes are read from its stylesheet in a hidden frame; on Android 12+ the widgets get both versions (`RemoteViews.setColorInt` and `setIcon`) so the home screen swaps them with dark mode. Self-updating is in `Updater.java`, `UpdateReceiver.java` and `UpdateJobService.java`; each build publishes `version.json` (build number and signing-key fingerprint) next to the APK. The **Android app** GitHub Actions workflow (`.github/workflows/android.yml`) builds the APK whenever `android-app/` changes and publishes it as the `android-latest` release. You can also run it by hand from the repository's **Actions** tab.

## Translation

In the Android app, translation runs on the phone with ML Kit: fast, unlimited and offline once a language is downloaded (about 30 MB, the first time that language is translated; scans are translated online meanwhile, and live translation waits for it). Elsewhere, translation runs on the phone where the browser offers a built-in translator (recent desktop Chrome). Otherwise it uses [MyMemory](https://mymemory.translated.net)'s free API, which needs no account. It allows roughly 5,000 characters a day per device, about a dozen menus. Past that, translation pauses until the next day; prices and currency detection keep working.

To get good translations the app:
- re-reads the photo with the text model for the detected language (French, Spanish, German, Japanese, Thai… about 40 languages), so accents and characters come out right;
- drops lines the text reader wasn't sure about, dot leaders and stray marks;
- translates item names printed over two lines as one phrase;
- only accepts MyMemory's stored human translations when they are for exactly that text, and otherwise uses its machine translation (its "fuzzy" matches for similar phrases are a common source of nonsense).

For a second opinion, **Open in Google Translate** sends the photo's text to Google Translate's website or app.

## Hosting it

The app is plain static files, so any HTTPS host works. HTTPS is required for the camera and for installing the app.

**GitHub Pages.** Free for public repositories; a private repository needs a paid GitHub plan.
1. Repository **Settings → Pages**.
2. Under *Build and deployment*, choose **Deploy from a branch**, pick the branch with this code and the `/ (root)` folder, then **Save**.
3. After a minute the app is live at `https://<username>.github.io/<repository>/`.

**Netlify or Cloudflare Pages.** Both are free and work with private repositories. Connect the repository, leave the build command empty and set the output directory to `/` (the repository root).

## Development

Requires Node.js 18 or newer. There are no dependencies to install.

```sh
npm start   # serves the app at http://localhost:8080
npm test    # runs the unit tests
```

The app always loads the newest files when online and uses its saved copy only offline; when a new service worker takes over, open pages reload themselves once.

### How the language is identified

The English text model reads digits and common currency signs in any photo, so prices come from it first. Text in another script comes out of that model as low-confidence nonsense. When that happens, the app tries the script models on a small copy of the photo, starting with the likeliest (from currency signs it saw and your "Prices in" setting), and keeps the one that reads the text best. It then reads the photo again with that model, which also picks up local currency words like 円. Latin-alphabet languages are identified from common words and accented letters.

## Icons

`icons/icon.svg` is the source: hollow green outlines on a transparent background. The PNGs are transparent too, except the Android "maskable" icon, which has a black background because Android crops home-screen icons into its own shape. iPhones show transparent home-screen icons on black.

## Exchange rates

Rates come from [ExchangeRate-API](https://www.exchangerate-api.com)'s free endpoint, with the open [Currency API](https://github.com/fawazahmed0/exchange-api) as a backup. Neither needs an API key. Rates update once a day and are mid-market rates, so banks and card providers may charge a little more.
